Breach records · three states, three clocks

Detection, assessment and notification are separate facts with separate times and separate actors, and the tenant sees all three. The statutory clock runs from assessment — three calendar days to the PDPC where it is notifiable, and affected individuals where significant harm is likely — which is why assessment is not allowed to be the same field as detection.

RecordOnDetectedAssessedNotifiedStanding

Who detects it

Under a customer-owned plane the data is in their account, so they may see it before we do — and detection carries who detected it for that reason. A tenant reports one by raising a case at the top severity; the case opens the record on our side. There is no separate reporting surface, because an incident nobody can find is worse than one raised through the door they already use.

Assessment is a judgement, and the thresholds are unverified

Whether a breach is notifiable, and whether significant harm is likely, are legal determinations. The timelines stated here are asserted from general knowledge and have not been verified against the current statute, which has been amended before. You cannot assess a breach without an audit trail — which is what makes the trail the thing that discharges the obligation rather than a nice-to-have.

Specified, not built. No record is written, no clock runs, and nothing notifies anyone.
Legal hold · the one statutory act we may reach, and only by breaking glass

Statutory acts are not the customer's to delegate — the obligation runs to the controller whatever a contract says. One exception exists: a regulator or court may order us, as processor, to preserve data inside a tenant, and an abandoned tenant has nobody left to place a hold while the retention sweep keeps purging.

Holds in force2 Placed by us, under order1 Named approvers3
Placing one, and what we still cannot do

Time-boxed, named approvers, heavily audited, and scoped to placing a hold only. Answering a data subject's request on the controller's behalf stays refused at any price, because standing is not something an emergency confers — making both break-glass would concede the point the refusal rests on. Preservation is not access: a hold suspends the sweep and grants no read, and separating those two is what makes the emergency defensible.

The argument against this, recorded rather than dropped

A class with one exception acquires a second, and the value of “in neither list, ever” is entirely in the word ever. It was overruled on the ground that a rule with no exception grows one informally, and an informal exception is unaudited. Both halves are worth keeping in view.

Specified, not built. No hold, no approver list, no suspension of the sweep, and no preservation order that grants no read.
