Routes and visibility

Which routes exist, which plane serves them, whether they are behind authentication, and who can see each one. Both of the surfaces that were the largest holes in the product are now specified — the staff console and the partner portal — so this tab is the authority on what each is allowed to be rather than a list of what is missing.

What is served from where, and whether it is protected

A route served from the data plane runs in the customer's own AWS account and never transits ours. A route served from the control plane runs in the 1CloudHub account and must never read tenant data — invariant 3.

The public plane — and it is a third plane nothing names

Every route in the table above is control-plane or data-plane. These two are neither. They are 1CloudHub-owned public web surfaces with no tenant, no session and no capability model — and the architecture documents do not describe a public plane at all, so nothing states who owns it, where it deploys, or what it is allowed to claim.

The G51 ruling

The contradiction was never between two decisions — it was between two readings of one word. G51 says no partner user signs in during v1. OC2 specifies a partner portal. Both were defensible, because "signs in" was doing two jobs: signing into the tenant, and signing into anything at all.

✅ Ruled: G51 governs the data plane, and only the data plane. No partner user ever authenticates against a customer's per-tenant host or reads a row of tenant data — that is the invariant worth protecting, and it is what makes resale safe to sell. The portal is a control-plane surface: rate cards, arrangements, the tenants a partner has sold, commission owed. All of it is control-plane data the partner already has a commercial right to see, and it lands in administration.openapi.yaml, which had to exist anyway for the staff console — a second audience on one surface rather than a second contract.

And it carries exactly one write. A deal registration (P9) is submitted by the partner and is a request until staff approve it, which is what keeps a visibility surface from quietly becoming a system partners can enter facts into. The control plane owns the registration; the CRM holds a copy.

What the ruling deliberately does not grant. Partner delivery still cannot support the tenant it sold, because that needs data-plane access and G51 correctly refuses it. Delegated tenant grants are not pursued — 1CloudHub is always the support path, priced into the partner deal. That makes the refusal a costed commercial term rather than an unserved role.

The partner journeys read against the ruling: P1, P2, P4, P5, P8 and P9 are v1, with everything but P9 executed by us; P3 and P6 are v1 — the invitation now has somewhere to arrive, and a partner can see its own commercial position; and P7 stays refused, costed rather than deferred. ⚠️ Two questions the registry created are open: precedence when two partners register the same deal, and expiry — without one the registry becomes a land-grab.

The handoffs

Ten places where a journey stops being one actor's and becomes another's. Every one of them is where onboarding actually stalls, and none of them exists as a tracked object today — which is why "why is this customer not live" has no answer.

1 · Provisioning consent 1CloudHub → cloud engineer

We hand over a CDK stack; they run it in their own account, with their mandatory resource tags applied. We cannot execute this and we cannot see whether it has been executed until the stack reports back.

Needs: a provisioning state per tenant, and a tag policy agreed before the stack runs rather than after — a stack deployed without the customer's tags is a chargeback hole that only surfaces on the first invoice.

2 · Key exchange cloud engineer → 1CloudHub

A KMS key pair is generated in their account; we register only the public half. The private half never leaves their boundary.

This is the handoff that makes the whole tenancy claim true, and it is one manual step with no state. If it is skipped, everything downstream appears to work until the first encrypted artefact.

3 · Model access pre-flight cloud engineer → 1CloudHub

Which Bedrock models, which region, which quotas, on-demand or provisioned. Under BYOA the inference bill is theirs, so this is a commercial conversation disguised as a configuration step.

The common stall: model access not enabled in their account, or a quota too low for the first close. Nothing in the product detects either until extraction fails.

4 · Identity federation cloud engineer → tenant administrator

Their IdP over OIDC or SAML, roles mapped to the product's roles. This is where the role model stops being ours — every journey's access depends on a mapping executed by someone who never signs into the product.

A wrong mapping is invisible: the user signs in successfully and is missing a destination.

5 · Connector credentials cloud engineer → the source system

Into their Secrets Manager. We never see one, which is the point — and which is why a bad credential is diagnosable only from the symptom.

The journey that suffers is C4, and its failure branch is a source that reads as unreachable with no way for us to tell whether the credential is wrong or the network is.

6 · Tag policy tenant administrator ↔ 1CloudHub

The cost-centre vocabulary, and which key drives chargeback. Genuinely joint: they own the vocabulary, we own what the meter does with it.

Agreed late, it invalidates every usage record emitted before the agreement — the one handoff whose lateness cannot be repaired retrospectively.

7 · First lease and heartbeat 1CloudHub → the data plane

The control plane issues the lease; the data plane confirms heartbeat and the usage stream. This is the moment the tenant becomes billable, and it is the only handoff the product can fully observe.

It is also the correct definition of "live" — and the reason onboarding needs a state per step is so that this and signed are never confused.

8 · Support access grant tenant administrator → 1CloudHub support

The support access mode is chosen at tenant creation, so how much we can see during an incident is their decision rather than ours.

⚠️ The trace half of this grant is unmintable — expressible as a grant, not issuable as a token. Support can be authorised for a read they cannot perform, and they find out during the incident.

9 · Partner tenant creation partner user → 1CloudHub → customer

A partner sells; we create, because no partner user can sign in. Defaults to hosted — BYOA under a partner is a deliberate escalation and pulls the customer's own cloud engineer into a deal the partner owns.

Three parties. ✅ The G51 ruling gives the partner a control-plane surface, but not this step — tenant creation stays ours, because four of its decisions have no defaults.

10 · Usage to invoice data plane → control plane → billing

Usage records ship up to the control plane and become an invoice against a rate card — wholesale to the partner, retail to the customer, or both.

⚠️ UsageRecord carries no UserId, so per-user analytics is impossible by construction. Any journey that assumes per-seat billing or per-user chargeback is unbuildable, not unbuilt.

What all ten have in common. Four onboarding steps are executed by a person who never signs into the product, two more are joint, and one becomes irreparable if it is agreed late. Onboarding is a pipeline with a state per step, not a checklist — and none of it exists in model/ today.

The nineteen rulings

What the map asked and what was decided. Each entry carries what the ruling cost, because a decision recorded without its consequence reads as free — and gets reopened by whoever meets the consequence first.

Nine came first and closed the product’s own gaps. The ten below closed the public plane, the partner surface, and the four questions the first nine created. What is left open is at the foot.

1 · The audit trail is a door C11 · unspecified → v1

A read-only /audit surface the assurance reader lands in, and the id every status bar prints resolves to a record — the run, the decisions on it, and who took them.

Cost. It would have been a fourth destination, and the shell rule says never a fourth. It is a door behind the avatar beside Setup instead, so R3 holds — and the assurance reader reads the register and the reports too, because a trail with no access to what it describes is a list of hashes.

2 · The whole staff console is specified H1 · H2 · H8 → v1

Tenancy, catalogue, rate cards, plans and onboarding state, all in administration.openapi.yaml. It was the largest v1 gap and nine journeys depended on it.

Cost. Specifying all five before building any is slower to first use than an onboarding-state-first slice. Every step stays manual for longer, and H1 says so in place.

3 · The user is the proposer of record C5 · vacuous → biting

Whoever triaged the exception proposed it. The existing segregation check finally has a name to compare against, and no eighth policy kind is needed — the defect was the missing proposer, not the missing policy.

Cost. This is the first version of the control that bites, and it bites the smallest customers: a one-person finance team cannot close at all.

4 · Segregation is a per-tenant policy C13 · a new journey

A tenant too small to staff two people may waive it knowingly, and every approval the waiver permits is recorded as self-approved.

Cost. The product now ships a control a customer can turn off, which is harder to say in a security review than one that is always on. The recording is what makes it defensible — the trail says which tenants waived it and what the waiver permitted.

5 · Telemetry gets a capability H5 · unmintable → issuable

The grant was expressible and the token was unissuable, so support was authorised for a read they could not perform — and found out during the incident.

Cost. None worth naming. This was a defect rather than a trade, and the customer's own support access mode still bounds how much of the trace is in scope.

6 · Reports is export plus scheduled delivery C7 · unspecified → v1

To email, their own S3, their SFTP, or a webhook. The tenant administrator owns the schedule; an user may schedule their own.

Cost. Report definitions, pinned answers and job history are out — and the controls for them should stop being drawn rather than stay hatched. It also creates a new failure mode: a schedule that fires with no run behind it delivers a confident empty report, on time. That needs a precondition nobody has specified yet.

7 · Delivery destinations are allow-listed C7 · C13

The tenant administrator approves destinations; an user may only schedule to one already approved.

Cost. An user who needs a new destination waits for an admin. That is the right trade — three of the four destination kinds leave the customer's account, and an user opening an egress path alone is the first thing a security review asks about.

8 · Migration moves everything, then tears down C12 · v2 → v1

Documents, runs, resolutions, approvals and the audit trail. No loss — a partial migration is a lost history wearing a better word. The old plane is decommissioned once the new one is verified, so no duplicate of regulated data lingers in an account we may no longer be provisioned in.

Cost. Tearing down on verification rather than on completion is what makes it recoverable, and it leaves a window where two planes hold the same period. Which one is authoritative during that window is not specified — the one thing this ruling left open.

9 · 1CloudHub is always the support path P7 · refused, and costed

Partner-led support stays refused and delegated tenant grants are not pursued. G51's invariant holds: no partner user reaches the data plane.

Cost. Every partner-sold incident routes through your support team, and it is priced into the deal. That makes the refusal honest rather than an unserved role — but partner support capacity now has to be planned against resale volume rather than discovered by it.

What is still open

Five, and four were created by the rulings themselves. That is normal and worth recording — a decision that creates no new question usually was not deciding much.

Registration precedence and expiry created by ruling 11

Two partners register the same deal and both believe they sourced it. Nothing specifies precedence — first to register, or first to bring the meeting.

And a deal registered and never closed holds the account forever. Without an expiry the registry becomes a land-grab, which is the failure mode every partner programme eventually meets.

The enumeration oracle accepted, not closed — ruling 15

A domain that resolves in the public lookup is a customer, and under BYOA a host name may carry their name. The allow-list narrows the oracle; it does not close it.

Answering only by email would close it completely. That trade was considered and the faster happy path was chosen — recorded here rather than discovered in a security review.

Support load from the allow-list created by ruling 15

Every contractor, subsidiary and consultant on a domain that is not the customer's becomes a support request before they can sign in.

It lands on support rather than on the tenant administrator, and it scales with the customer's org chart rather than with usage — which is the wrong axis for a support cost to grow along.

Invoice reproducibility created by ruling 19

Effective dates without versioning means an old invoice may not be reproducible from the current rate card. The adjustment reason is the only record of why the two disagree.

Per-user attribution · the hosted frontend pre-existing, and unchanged

Per-user attribution is unbuildable rather than unbuilt: UsageRecord ships with no UserId, so any journey assuming per-seat billing cannot be built without changing the record. It also bounds what a partner dispute can ever be answered with.

The customer-hosted frontend is H10's branch — the app is served from our CloudFront and executes against their data in their browser. The data path is clean and the code path is ours, and it is the one BYOA question the delivery model cannot answer yes to.

The reconciliation · 18 Sep 2026

The three families were read against the design records — data architecture, day-2 operations, governance, the access model, the injection posture and the channel research. Seven journeys disagreed with a record, and one record disagrees with this page. Both directions are written down.

The role vocabulary was three vocabularies applied everywhere

user · administrator · author · sponsor · assurance. operator is gone — it has no standing in ITIL, COBIT, TOGAF or NIST, and in a technical room it means a Kubernetes controller. auditor became assurance, which is the word the IIA, Basel, the OCC, MAS and ISACA all use for independent non-operating verification. Partner roles became partner administrator and partner user. Approver stays — it names a capability holder rather than a stakeholder class.

Two classes became real roles rather than names. Assurance reads the trail, the governance console, closed periods of the register and policy with its amendment history — and never live work, because an auditor watching an open queue is watching people work. The sponsor holds one act: acknowledging a period.

Break-glass was rebuilt the same day it was decided H5 · reversed

The afternoon's ruling was a self-issued 24-hour grant with a typed reason and no second approver. The access record had already decided the opposite in August, and the grant type re-checks the two-person rule when the grant is used, not only when it is issued — so the ruling was not implementable against the model that exists.

What replaced it: the customer authorises at signing — purpose, resource classes, a ceiling on hours, who may invoke, the notification path — and each incident draws against it with a second 1CloudHub person approving. Nobody at the customer is woken and nobody waits on their change control. ⚠️ The cost is a rota: at 03:00 on a Sunday, two people awake. That is an operational commitment and it should be priced before it is promised.

What we may reach, and what arrives anyway H1 · H5 · a second axis

The support access mode was doing two jobs. It now sits beside a telemetry profile — minimum, standard, full — because one says what we may reach on request and the other says what arrives continuously, without asking. minimum is a floor nobody goes below: it carries no customer content and is the smallest set a failure can be classified from, so it belongs in the SLA rather than in a negotiation.

The pair has a consequence worth contracting: minimum plus support access never is a customer supportable at error-class triage and a customer-run bundle and no further. Legitimate, and not a thing to discover during an incident.

Five journeys nobody had drawn C · governance · new

Subject access, correction, erasure, breach and legal hold were all modelled and none was on this page. They now sit in a governance destination on the tenant surface, because the acts are the tenant's: answering a request and running an erasure are theirs to perform, and assurance reads the result.

The subject is usually not a user. In KYC the person whose passport was verified has no account and cannot be named by a user id, so requests arrive through the tenant keyed by an opaque reference. An erasure claims erased, unqualified — residuals are a contractual disclosure rather than a screen. ⚠️ The subject index reaches database rows only: exports, page renders and traces are outside it, and a copy that exists only in a backup has no disposition at all.

Two of the five are ours and are named rather than drawn. Breach is three states with three clocks and a detector — the statutory clock runs from assessment, and under a customer-owned plane the tenant may detect first. Legal hold is the one statutory act we may reach, by break-glass, scoped to placing a hold and never to answering a request on the controller's behalf.

The lease is disclosed, or it is a catastrophe C8 · H6

A signed 30-day entitlement, drawn down locally, with a 7-day grace — on the plan surface with its dates, because a kill switch in someone's account is a trust catastrophe if it is discovered rather than disclosed. The ceiling is a multiple of the tier, not equal to it: equal numbers would stop a month-end close over a commercial limit.

The ladder is stated: warned, new runs refused, read-only. A run in flight always completes and export and the trail are never blocked. That settles the in-flight question rather than leaving it to a preference.

The door that stays shut, drawn open P7 · refused, and evidenced

The governance record has a per-customer opt-in reaching a partner into exceptions. P7 stays refused and the grant is the exception that proves it — drawn in the portal with nobody holding one, granted only by a named person at the customer, time-bounded and revocable. A live grant almost certainly makes the partner a sub-processor, which their customer's own contract has to permit. Absence of a grant is what keeps a partner a reseller.

Two ways an address resolves V3 · H11

Federation sits beside the allow-list rather than replacing it: a registered directory routes by DNS-verified domain, and everyone else resolves against the list we keep. A directory asserts membership, not employment, so a guest in a customer's directory is refused and sent to its own realm — otherwise a partner's engineer arrives as that customer's own person. And because a directory knows who someone is now rather than who they were then, the organisation is stamped on every act at the time it happens.

Where this page disagrees with a record G36 · unreconciled, by ruling

G36 slips BYOA to v1+1. Two records carry banners saying their own premise is reversed by it, and model/deployment.ts is dropped for v1 whole — which would take the fleet, the heartbeat, liveness, engine and schema versions with it.

This page keeps BYOA in v1 by ruling: the first non-demo customer is a regulated enterprise on a customer-owned plane. So C12 and P8 stand, the partner sandbox stays a tenant in the partner's own account, and half the tenants in the staff console are BYOA. ⚠️ The disagreement is recorded rather than resolved, and it is the one place a reader should check which document they are trusting.
